# Chapter 2: Background and Literature Review

**Target length:** 4,000–5,000 words

## 2.1 The Rise of LLM Benchmarking

The evaluation of natural language processing systems has a history considerably older than the systems it now primarily assesses. Early evaluation in NLP relied on narrow, well-specified tasks — part-of-speech tagging, syntactic parsing, information extraction — where ground truth was largely unambiguous and performance could be measured against annotated corpora with precision. The shift toward neural language models over the first two decades of the twenty-first century gradually eroded the sufficiency of these task-specific metrics. As models became capable of producing coherent text across many domains, researchers needed comparative frameworks that could characterise model capability in aggregate rather than in isolation.

The introduction of the General Language Understanding Evaluation (GLUE) benchmark in 2018 (Wang et al., 2018) and its successor SuperGLUE (Wang et al., 2019) marked a turning point. These benchmarks collected multiple existing tasks — natural language inference, coreference resolution, question answering, sentiment analysis — into unified evaluation suites and provided a single composite score that allowed researchers to compare models across a wide capability surface. The value of this approach was immediate: GLUE scores became a standard reporting mechanism in NLP papers, and a model's GLUE ranking became a proxy for its general linguistic competence. The limitation was equally immediate. Models trained specifically to perform well on GLUE quickly saturated the benchmark, with systems exceeding human performance on SuperGLUE within approximately three years of the benchmark's publication (He et al., 2021). Saturation was not evidence of genuine parity with human language ability; it was evidence of benchmark-specific optimisation.

The release of GPT-3 in 2020 (Brown et al., 2020) changed the terms of the evaluation challenge in a more fundamental way. GPT-3 and the large-scale models that followed demonstrated strong performance across an open-ended range of tasks without task-specific fine-tuning, which meant that evaluation frameworks designed around narrow tasks could no longer serve as a complete account of what models could do. The Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) benchmark, introduced by Hendrycks et al. (2021), responded to this by collecting 57 academic subject areas — from elementary mathematics to professional medicine and law — and framing evaluation as multiple-choice question answering over this broad knowledge domain. MMLU became rapidly adopted as a reporting standard in model technical reports and academic papers, and it remains one of the most widely cited benchmark scores in LLM evaluation as of 2025 (Singh et al., 2025).

The BIG-Bench (Srivastava et al., 2022) effort extended the multi-task principle further, assembling more than 200 tasks across capabilities including arithmetic, commonsense reasoning, translation, coding, and novel problem types. BIG-Bench Hard (Suzgun et al., 2022) subsequently filtered these to the subset most challenging for contemporary models, directing evaluation pressure toward areas where models most clearly fell short. Alongside these general-purpose benchmarks, domain-specific evaluation frameworks proliferated. HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) provided a standardised coding benchmark through 164 Python function completion problems, enabling direct comparison of model performance on code generation. GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) established a standard for elementary mathematical word problem solving. The HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), ARC (Clark et al., 2018), and TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022) benchmarks addressed commonsense inference, scientific knowledge, and truthfulness respectively.

By the mid-2020s, public leaderboards had become central to how models were compared and how research priorities were set. The Open LLM Leaderboard, Chatbot Arena, and the leaderboards maintained by individual model providers presented ranking tables based on aggregated benchmark scores, and these rankings influenced which models received commercial adoption, which capabilities received research attention, and which papers received acceptance at major venues. The practical consequence of this centralisation was substantial: the benchmark ecosystem had become a de facto standard for communicating model capability, making the validity of that ecosystem a matter of practical significance rather than merely academic interest.

## 2.2 Empirical Studies of Real-World LLM Use

Despite the dominance of benchmark-based evaluation, empirical study of how people actually use LLMs was, until recently, sparse. Most early work on user behaviour relied on small survey instruments or limited observational samples that could not support generalisable quantitative claims about the distribution of use cases. This changed as large-scale usage data became available from major providers.

The most significant study for the present project is that of Handa et al. (2025), who analysed approximately four million conversations between users and Claude.ai, Anthropic's commercial assistant product, mapping each conversation to task categories drawn from the Occupational Information Network (O\*NET) Detailed Work Activities database. The O\*NET system, maintained by the United States Department of Labor, provides a structured taxonomy of work activities that span the range of occupational roles in the American economy, from routine manual tasks to highly specialised professional functions. By matching Claude.ai conversations to O\*NET categories, Handa et al. produced the most comprehensive quantitative picture of real-world LLM usage available in the academic literature at the time of this project.

Their analysis revealed three findings with direct bearing on the evaluation question. First, usage is heavily concentrated: technical assistance, which includes software development, debugging, and system configuration, accounts for approximately 65.1% of recorded usage. Second, reviewing and improving work — editing, critiquing, proofreading, and reformatting existing artefacts — accounts for approximately 58.9% of usage, making it the second most common pattern of interaction. Third, information retrieval and summarisation, tasks more closely aligned with traditional question-answering benchmarks, each account for approximately 16.6% of usage. The Anthropic Economic Index (AEI) February 2026 update extends this analysis and provides the primary usage frequency data used in the present project, reporting usage percentages across the top 103 task categories in the current snapshot.

A complementary dataset is provided by Ouyang et al. (2025), who analyse usage of OpenAI's assistant platform through an NBER working paper examining millions of API requests. Their findings confirm several patterns from the Handa et al. analysis while adding a different commercial platform's perspective. Writing, coding, analysis, and tutoring emerge as dominant categories, with individual task frequencies broadly consistent with those from the Anthropic data. This cross-platform consistency is significant: it suggests that the usage patterns identified in the Anthropic data are not idiosyncratic to a particular model or platform but reflect more general patterns of user demand.

The OpenRouter 100T Token Study contributes a third perspective drawn from a multi-model platform that includes substantial traffic from open-source model deployments. This source reveals that roleplay and interactive fiction account for a disproportionately large share of token consumption in open-source contexts, a pattern less visible in the enterprise-oriented Anthropic data. Together, these three sources — commercial enterprise usage, commercial consumer usage, and open-source usage — provide a more complete picture of the range of tasks that users actually bring to LLM systems.

Xu et al. (2025) offer a different form of empirical evidence: a benchmark specifically designed to evaluate LLM performance on realistic workplace tasks rather than on academic exercise formats. TheAgentCompany simulates a software company environment and requires LLM agents to complete tasks drawn from human resources, finance, software engineering, and administrative workflows. The finding that state-of-the-art models complete approximately 30% of these tasks, despite strong scores on conventional benchmarks, constitutes direct evidence of the gap between benchmark performance and practical capability. The TheAgentCompany results are particularly notable because they involve the same frontier models that achieve near-ceiling performance on MMLU, GPQA, and HumanEval, demonstrating that aggregate benchmark strength does not predict performance on integrated, multi-step workplace tasks.

Collectively, these empirical studies establish that the tasks users most frequently bring to LLM systems — technical assistance, reviewing work, information retrieval, education support, and data analysis — are not fully represented in the dominant evaluation frameworks. The mismatch is not a matter of degree but of kind: reviewing and improving work, for example, occupies nearly 59% of usage in the Handa et al. analysis but has no dedicated evaluation framework that addresses its distinctive requirements as a primary construct.

## 2.3 Benchmark Validity and Known Limitations

Alongside the empirical evidence of usage patterns, a substantial body of work has documented specific validity problems in the benchmark ecosystem. These problems are distinct from the coverage gap identified in this project — they concern not only which capabilities are evaluated but whether the evaluations that exist are measuring what they claim to measure.

Data contamination is among the most extensively documented validity threats. Balloccu et al. (2024) conduct a systematic examination of data contamination in closed-source models, showing that benchmark test items appear in model training data at rates that inflate reported performance. The difficulty of diagnosing contamination in closed-source systems, where training data is not publicly disclosed, means that contamination risk is often unknown rather than absent. The consequence for users of benchmark scores is that reported performance may reflect memorisation of specific items rather than generalised capability: a model may score 90% on a benchmark while failing on functionally equivalent problems that were not in its training distribution.

Jain et al. (2024) provide a particularly compelling demonstration of contamination effects in the coding domain. LiveCodeBench, which they introduce, collects programming problems from competitive coding platforms organised by the date of their public release. By comparing model performance on problems released before and after likely training cutoff dates, Jain et al. show that performance drops substantially on post-cutoff problems, even for tasks of nominally similar difficulty. This temporal performance gap constitutes direct evidence that strong scores on established coding benchmarks such as HumanEval reflect, at least in part, familiarity with specific problems rather than general programming ability. The implication is that benchmark scores computed on static, widely distributed test sets are systematically upward-biased for models with recent training data.

Format sensitivity introduces a further validity problem. Pezeshkpour and Hruschka (2024) demonstrate that changing the order of answer options in multiple-choice questions can shift model rankings by approximately eight positions on MMLU-style evaluations. Since the content of the question and the set of possible answers remain unchanged, the correct answer remains equally valid regardless of its position, and a model with genuine knowledge should respond identically regardless of option ordering. The observed ranking instability reveals that models are sensitive to surface-level presentation features rather than — or in addition to — the underlying knowledge content the benchmark is intended to measure. This finding has direct implications for the use of multiple-choice benchmarks as selection criteria: the model ranked highest on a given leaderboard might rank lower had options been presented in a different order.

Selective disclosure introduces a further distortion between raw model performance and reported rankings. Singh et al. (2025) examine the systematic practices by which benchmark results are reported in technical reports and press releases, showing that companies selectively cite the benchmarks on which their models perform best while omitting or downplaying benchmarks on which they perform less well. Their analysis of the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Leaderboard shows that including all available benchmark results rather than provider-selected subsets can shift rankings by up to 112 positions. This finding means that leaderboard comparisons based on reported scores are not comparable on equal terms: the benchmarks included in a given technical report are themselves a strategic selection rather than a representative sample.

The concept of Goodhart's Law applies with particular force to LLM benchmarking. Goodhart (1975), as elaborated by Strathern (1997), states that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure: optimising directly for a metric tends to decouple it from the underlying construct it was intended to proxy. In the context of LLM evaluation, this principle manifests in at least two forms. First, models are increasingly trained and fine-tuned with awareness of the benchmark landscape, directing learning pressure toward benchmark-specific performance rather than capability development. Second, researchers select benchmarks partly on the basis of where their models perform well, creating a feedback loop in which the benchmarks that report the highest scores receive the most attention and the benchmarks that report the lowest scores are less prominent in the literature. Chang et al. (2023) review these dynamics in a comprehensive survey of LLM evaluation, noting that the field faces a structural tendency to over-optimise for measurable proxies at the expense of genuine capability development.

Benchmark saturation — the progressive erosion of discriminability as models approach ceiling performance on a given test set — compounds these problems. MMLU, HumanEval, and many other benchmarks originally designed to challenge state-of-the-art models have seen performance levels reach or approach ceiling for leading models as of 2024 and 2025. This saturation means that differences between top models on these benchmarks are often within the margin of statistical uncertainty, rendering them poor instruments for fine-grained comparison. The response in the research community has been to introduce harder variants — MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024), HumanEval+ (Liu et al., 2023), GPQA Diamond (Rein et al., 2023), and Humanity's Last Exam (Phan et al., 2025) — which restore discriminability but maintain the underlying task-type structure of their predecessors.

Static benchmarks with fixed test sets face a compounding problem over time: the same items may be encountered by increasingly many models, increasing contamination risk with each additional release cycle. Dynamic benchmarks — those that introduce new items periodically, such as LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2024) and LiveBench (White et al., 2024) — address this concern directly, but they introduce challenges of longitudinal comparability: performance on a dynamically refreshed benchmark is not directly comparable across time unless the difficulty distribution of new items is carefully controlled.

## 2.4 Existing Capability Taxonomies

Prior to this project, several attempts had been made to organise the landscape of LLM capabilities into structured taxonomies. These efforts varied in their empirical grounding, their intended application, and the granularity of their categorisation.

The O\*NET Detailed Work Activities (DWA) system, developed and maintained by the United States Department of Labor through the National Center for O\*NET Development, provides a comprehensive taxonomy of work-relevant task categories spanning the full range of occupational activities in the American economy. O\*NET DWAs are granular and occupationally anchored, which makes them well-suited to mapping LLM usage to economic and labour-market frameworks, as in the Handa et al. (2025) study. However, the O\*NET system was not designed for the purpose of evaluating AI capabilities, and many of its categories reflect occupational role distinctions that do not map cleanly onto the interaction modes relevant for LLM evaluation. A taxonomy designed for occupational classification may, for example, distinguish tasks by the professional domain in which they occur — medicine, law, engineering — rather than by the cognitive function the LLM performs, which is the more relevant distinction for evaluation purposes.

Ouyang et al. (2025) present a usage-derived taxonomy in their analysis of OpenAI platform traffic. Their categorisation scheme identifies writing, coding, analysis, and tutoring as major usage categories, with subcategories distinguishing different types of writing tasks (creative, professional, academic) and different types of coding activity. This scheme is broadly aligned with the capabilities identified in this project, but it is not formally defined with decision rules, its inter-coder reliability is not reported, and it is not systematically linked to the benchmark inventory it would imply.

Chatterji et al. (2025) develop a capability taxonomy in the context of evaluating model fitness for enterprise deployment, distinguishing categories such as knowledge retrieval, reasoning, generation, and following instructions. Their taxonomy is researcher-constructed rather than derived from usage data, and it does not address the full range of interaction modes visible in large-scale usage datasets, particularly the absence of review-and-feedback and tutoring as distinct constructs.

The OpenRouter 100T Token Study provides usage frequency data across a multi-model platform that is particularly strong on roleplay and open-source model use, reflecting the distinctive character of the non-commercial LLM user population. This source does not present a formal taxonomy but provides category distributions that are useful for cross-validating taxonomies derived from commercial usage data.

The academic literature on LLM evaluation more broadly has not converged on a shared capability taxonomy. Review papers such as Chang et al. (2023) and Liang et al. (2022) organise capabilities into broad clusters — language understanding, knowledge, reasoning, generation — but these clusters are researcher-defined and reflect the structure of the existing benchmark landscape rather than empirically documented patterns of user demand. The consequence is that the majority of evaluation taxonomies in the literature are shaped by what is easy to benchmark rather than by what users actually do.

Several evaluation frameworks explicitly focus on agentic capabilities — the ability of models to plan, use tools, and execute multi-step tasks autonomously. GAIA (Mialon et al., 2023) and the AgentBench family of benchmarks address this mode of operation, and TheAgentCompany (Xu et al., 2025) extends it to realistic workplace settings. These frameworks capture a dimension of LLM behaviour that is increasingly important as models are deployed in autonomous agent configurations, and they are included in the benchmark inventory of this project. However, agentic behaviour is a modality that cuts across multiple capability areas rather than constituting a distinct capability in its own right: an agent may be executing a software development task (C02), an information retrieval task (C03), or a data analysis task (C07) while using tools and multi-step planning. The taxonomy developed in this project treats agentic operation as a characteristic of task execution rather than as a separate capability category.

## 2.5 Gaps in the Literature

The review of the preceding four sections reveals a consistent gap in the existing literature: no prior study provides a systematic, usage-weighted, cross-benchmark coverage analysis that links empirically documented usage frequencies to structured quality assessments of whether existing benchmarks actually measure the capabilities users most demand.

Individual critiques of specific benchmarks are numerous and often well-documented. Jain et al. (2024) have identified contamination problems in coding benchmarks; Pezeshkpour and Hruschka (2024) have demonstrated format sensitivity in multiple-choice evaluations; Singh et al. (2025) have analysed selective disclosure in leaderboard reporting; and Balloccu et al. (2024) have characterised contamination risks in closed-source models. Each of these is a valid and practically significant finding, but each addresses a single dimension of a single type of benchmark problem. No work integrates these concerns into a framework that prioritises them according to usage frequency, that maps the full range of documented capabilities against the full population of major benchmarks, or that produces actionable gap scores to direct new benchmark development.

Empirical usage studies have documented what users do with LLMs. Handa et al. (2025), Ouyang et al. (2025), and the OpenRouter data collectively establish the distribution of real-world demand across capability types. But these studies do not analyse the evaluation implications of their findings: they do not identify which usage capabilities have no corresponding benchmark framework, nor do they quantify the severity of coverage shortfalls relative to demand.

Benchmark surveys and evaluation reviews — including Chang et al. (2023), Liang et al. (2022), and the Holistic Evaluation of Language Models (HELM) framework (Liang et al., 2022) — document the landscape of available benchmarks and identify desirable properties for evaluation. But they do not ground their analyses in empirical usage data, and their capability categorisation schemes reflect the existing benchmark structure rather than challenging it. A researcher consulting these surveys would learn what benchmarks exist and what they measure, but not how well the benchmark ecosystem as a whole addresses the capabilities users most need.

This project addresses the gap between these bodies of work. By building a capability taxonomy from usage data before examining the benchmark landscape, it ensures that the categories used to assess coverage are driven by user demand rather than by evaluation convenience. By computing usage-weighted gap scores that combine demand frequency with coverage quality, it produces a prioritised assessment of where new evaluation frameworks are most urgently needed. And by producing actionable benchmark design specifications and a reusable practitioner toolkit, it provides the research community with outputs that can directly inform the next generation of LLM evaluation development.
